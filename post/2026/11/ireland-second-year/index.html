Studying, Working and Living in Ireland - Year Two

After one year of study and one year of work: jobs, housing, food, income, visas, parcels and getting around in Dublin

Nov 16, 2026
7 minute read
中文

I came to Dublin, Ireland to study in July 2024 and started working in October of the following year. It has now been two years: one year of study and one year of work.

I previously wrote a post about studying here: https://nansenli.com/post/2025/04/ireland-study-guide/. This one is about work and life.

Work

From my own experience, finding a job in Ireland is about as hard as in China, or a bit harder. Among the people around me, I haven’t seen jobs being generally easy to find, nor have I seen many people unable to find one. Compared with the jobs these people had found back in China, things are roughly on par. The industries I’m talking about are mainly finance, computing, and general work such as catering and tutoring.

So when estimating how hard it will be to find a job in Ireland, you can use how hard it would be for you in China as a reference, and then make it a bit harder. In particular, as a foreigner you face a higher bar in hiring to begin with. In other words, many companies still won’t hire foreigners.

Many companies in Ireland allow remote work, so you don’t necessarily need to go to the office every day. This is a very attractive point, provided the company culture allows it.

Multinationals usually have offices around the world and attract and select talent globally, so online meetings are very common, which makes working from home possible. Commuting in Ireland is also a problem in itself, which makes forcing tech and finance staff to work on-site seem rather inhumane.

This is very different from China. Public transport there is more developed, the rental market is larger and more flexible, there’s no way to fully trust everyone’s work quality when working from home, and competition is fiercer, so working in the office is the norm.

If you want to go abroad to escape the rat race in China and hope for better job prospects, you may need to check carefully whether your industry really has better prospects than at home.

Housing

Rent in Ireland is generally high. A single person needs roughly €1,000–1,400 to live somewhere convenient and decent. Housing quality is quite polarised, similar to China: worse places tend to be old and smell of mould, while better and newer places are more modern, such as newly built apartments.

I personally highly recommend living in a newer apartment. A two-bedroom is usually around €3,000, i.e. €1,500 per person. However, before you find a proper job, you have no income, which makes it hard to get an apartment. So you need to handle the transition from student housing to working-life housing carefully.

As a student, you can rent from a Chinese landlord or live in student accommodation. Student accommodation is only available to students, so it’s a kind of subsidised housing.

If your budget is very limited and you also need to commute into the city, it gets very hard. I once tried to find a two-bedroom in the city with good transport links on a budget of about €1,200 per person. It was very difficult. I did eventually find one, but it smelled quite mouldy and wasn’t well insulated.

Insulation is a real issue for Irish homes. New builds usually have better insulation and ventilation. With poor insulation, heating in winter becomes a big expense, and working from home is uncomfortable too.

Overall, to find a place you’re happy with, you either pay more, or accept that it’s hard to find, far from transport, or old. Good places also require you to have a stable status and job, with proof and documents to submit; otherwise commercial apartment operators won’t rent to people they see as high-risk. So the first period after arriving in Ireland can be very difficult.

Food

In Ireland, buying groceries and cooking is completely normal. Eating out usually costs €8–20 per person. This means the kind of model in China where you just eat downstairs, or the even more advanced Singapore hawker centre model, simply doesn’t exist. It isn’t sustainable: eating out occasionally is fine, but doing it every day would bankrupt you sooner or later. Likewise, takeaway is even more expensive because of delivery fees. It’s only for celebrations or the occasional treat.

Food is usually bought at big supermarkets such as Lidl, Tesco or Dunnes. However, the selection is limited to fairly fixed categories. For example, beef is mostly ribeye, sirloin and similar cuts.

That said, Dublin has a very good-value supermarket called 融兴行 (Rong Xing Hang). It offers online ordering with same-day delivery, and delivery is free on orders over €30. Its range is also richer, for example many different cuts of meat such as beef short ribs. It started out in the fishing business, so its seafood is worth recommending too.

Income

Taxes in Ireland are generally high. Because of the minimum wage, income gaps aren’t as wide as in China. In China, among programmers, some may earn ¥3,000 a month while others earn ¥50,000 — about 16 times as much. In Ireland the gap isn’t that large. Monthly salaries are usually between €3,000 and €8,000.

Say a typical job pays €60,000 a year. That’s about €3,500 a month after tax, or €120 a day to spend. With rent at €1,200, that’s €40 a day, leaving €80. Electricity for normal showering is at least €2 a day. Internet, phone and commuting also cost money; call it €10 altogether. That leaves roughly €50–60 a day. If you eat well, for example plenty of beef, eggs and milk, food is fairly expensive, around €15 a day. That leaves about €40 a day in savings — roughly 30% of take-home pay.

So how big is the income gap really? We can work it out: living in China versus abroad, what share of income do living costs take, and if the savings ratio were the same, what total income in China would that correspond to? Here each side is calculated using its most common lifestyle: in China, long overtime hours, cheap takeaway and basically no cooking; in Ireland, finishing work early, expensive takeaway and cooking for yourself.

Take Chengdu: for a single person sharing a flat, a nicer room costs about ¥1,600, near a big shopping centre with a Hema supermarket. Electricity ¥200, phone ¥30, internet ¥100 — about ¥2,000 in total. Takeaway every day costs about ¥30, so ¥1,000 a month, ¥3,000 in total. If the savings ratio after expenses is also 30% of after-tax income, that works out to about ¥4,300 a month after tax — a job paying around ¥5,000 a month before tax in Chengdu.

For Shenzhen, with monthly expenses of ¥6,000, that’s equivalent to about ¥8,600 after tax, or a little over ¥10,000 before tax.

Or here’s a more realistic example. Someone I know earned ¥400,000 a year in Shanghai, and after coming to Dublin landed a job paying €70,000 a year.

This means that if your pre-tax income in China is already fairly high, then unless you land a particularly well-paid job in Dublin, your disposable income will actually drop if you want to maintain your quality of life. Many people expect “going abroad = earning more + getting residency”. In reality, for people already well paid in China, you can usually only have one of the two: you get the residency, but you earn somewhat less.

Visas and Residency

Ireland’s immigration pathway is fairly clear. The student permission is Stamp 2; after graduating you can apply for Stamp 1G; after that comes the work permission Stamp 1; and once you meet the conditions you can apply for Stamp 4.

Note that for every residence card, you must prepare to replace or renew it 3 months before it expires, and plan your travel around that. If your card is about to expire and the new one hasn’t arrived, your travel plans may be affected. Also, the renewal website is constantly delayed due to technical issues or queues, so plan ahead.

For example, if your Stamp 1 expires on 1 September, you should apply to renew on 1 June. If you still haven’t received the renewed card by 1 August, you can’t plan any trips after September.

Some friends have also had their applications stuck in review, or had renewals rejected with requests for more documents, so leave plenty of time. Also make sure your passport has at least one year of validity when you renew, otherwise the period granted may be too short and you’ll have to renew again. So renew your passport in good time.

Furthermore, after graduating you use Stamp 1G to look for work, but your company may be slow to process Stamp 1 or the Critical Skills Employment Permit, and may only do it at the last minute. Don’t assume the company will definitely get it done smoothly — even big companies can take a long time because of queues and the like. In addition, the Critical Skills Employment Permit (CSEP) restricts changing employers; you generally need to stay with your first employer for a certain period before you can switch.

Also, the immigration office sometimes issues the wrong type of permission, in which case you’ll need to contact them again to reapply. Patience is required.

And although Ireland’s labour policies have always been fairly stable, they could change, especially as the world is turning towards conservatism, so keep an eye on policy changes.

Parcels

In Dublin, online shopping isn’t part of everyday life. After ordering on Amazon or other platforms, it usually takes at least three to five days for things to arrive. Express delivery can speed it up. Temu or Shein take about 10 days. Same-day or next-day delivery like in China or with JD.com doesn’t exist.

That doesn’t mean you can’t buy from Taobao or JD, though. You can use a parcel consolidation service: they have a warehouse, you buy things online, for example on Taobao or JD, and have them delivered to the warehouse. Once you reach 11 kg you can ship, and it arrives in Ireland in about 40 days. Shipping costs about ¥500–700 each time. So basically you spend a month buying and accumulating things, ship once you reach the weight, and on average you can shop online like this three or four times a year. I usually use a company called 有易集运 (Youyi), which has been very reliable. I’ve never had a problem with packing, shipping or receiving. Their warehouse is in Shanghai, which makes receiving goods easy. I’ve shipped with them about 7 or 8 times so far and they’ve never let me down.

Besides long waits and high prices, another factor is that receiving parcels is a hassle. In Ireland couriers don’t deliver on Saturdays and Sundays, so deliveries only happen Monday to Friday — when you may be at work. Also, unlike China with its pickup stations everywhere, Irish deliveries require a signature. This means someone has to be home to sign. Even if you are home, couriers sometimes don’t call or knock and simply mark the delivery as failed. If a delivery fails, the parcel is sent to a depot, usually far from where you live, so you’ll need to spend about two hours specifically going to collect it. Sometimes parcels are just left in some corner without notice. There’s also a small chance a parcel gets lost. So keep a good attitude.

UK Visa and Schengen Visa

Ireland is not part of the Schengen Area, so studying and working in Ireland on a Chinese passport only covers Ireland. Generally, you can travel around Ireland in your first six months, after which you’ll need to think about travelling elsewhere, because many Irish attractions aren’t worth repeat visits.

I highly recommend getting a UK visa. Applying for one from Ireland isn’t complicated. The most annoying part of applying for any visa is booking an appointment, and luckily UK visa appointments are plentiful — you can usually book one within two weeks. The validity is also basically whatever you pay for: apply for 2 years and you get 2 years. Apart from being a bit expensive, there’s nothing to complain about. With Schengen visas, the validity granted is random.

Besides the UK, you’ll also want a Schengen visa. Getting one from Ireland is extremely hard. First, appointments are very hard to get. Second, the validity granted is usually short: your first Schengen visa may only cover a few days, the second about 1 month, and the third about 3 months. On top of that, processing times after submission vary; some consulates take about a month to issue the visa, which means you can’t use your passport for a month.

Different Schengen countries differ in processing time, appointment difficulty, application channels and validity granted, so choosing and booking is very complicated. The French visa is affixed in Dublin and processed quickly, but the appointment queue is very long. The Danish embassy handles visas for several Nordic countries; appointments are easy to get, but processing often takes about a month. For other countries you have to grab a slot when one opens up.

So if you have travel plans, preparing 3 months in advance is best. For trips within 2 months or 1 month, think carefully. First, it’s best to get a Schengen visa and use it successfully — that is, use it once honestly according to the itinerary you applied with — and after that you can use it to visit other Schengen countries. For example, if you want to visit Schengen country A, the advice is to keep queuing for a French visa in the meantime; if you get it, plan a trip combining France with your intended destination.

If you don’t want to queue, you’ll need to grab slots, for example for Spanish or Danish visas.

If you really can’t get a Schengen appointment yourself, you can consider an agent. Agents can usually get a slot within 1–2 weeks, and processing takes about another 1–2 weeks. Add the 2 weeks of buffer you should leave before travelling. So plan your Schengen visa early and don’t procrastinate, or you’ll end up going nowhere.

Also watch the validity of your IRP card and passport. A Schengen visa can’t extend beyond the validity of your IRP card and passport, so before applying, try to make sure both have already been renewed.

Getting Around the City

For buses, Irish buses currently don’t accept mobile payment; you need a transport card, so you must carry it when you go out. Buses are infrequent: high-frequency routes come about every 15 minutes, low-frequency ones maybe only once an hour, and if you miss it, that’s it. Some stops have real-time information displays, and you can also download TFI Live or use Google Maps to check bus locations in real time. Inside the bus there are no electronic displays or route indicators and no estimated arrival times; you can only check on your phone. Some routes, such as the E1, can stop for quite long periods. As for the network, Dublin’s buses radiate from the city centre in a star pattern, supplemented by various orbital routes. Routes are sparse, so getting to the city centre, shopping centres and interchanges is convenient, but travelling between other areas is difficult — no direct routes, long waits and so on.

Taxis in Dublin are quite convenient; you can use Free Now or Uber and get a car quickly. However, they’re expensive, so they’re generally best for short trips where there’s no bus.

As for driving, I personally highly recommend it, as it solves many inconveniences. Unfortunately I haven’t bought a car yet — I’ll share more once I have one.
